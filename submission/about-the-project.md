# About the project

Devpost story field. Markdown, no headings, freeflowing by design. Built from Sri's own hook draft, kept as close to his sentences as possible.

---

Every time I switched between building my startup and researching what I need to do to prepare for freshman year at Stanford, while using the same frontier models at my disposal, I was hit in the face with a sharp divergence in performance. The same model that built an entire secure backend meant to withstand ten thousand users and connected it to a frontend without trouble (the CORS errors alone took me hours just 3 years ago), was reduced to a useless machine awkwardly misclicking around Stanford Axess trying to retrieve any piece of information at all. It searched from stale sources and did not know what to do with broken links. It misclicked and accidentally closed browsing sessions. It forgot everything about me in a matter of seconds. I saw that consumer AI was nowhere near the frontier I knew in development, and the problem was context and browsing.

Which is why, when I heard that WebMCP, a concept I had heard of but never seen working, was actually moving forward, I was thrilled. This hackathon came at the right time, and with it the idea to finally streamline the process I had been struggling with all summer: pointing frontier intelligence at possibly the most important planning task of my adult life. I named it Acorn, an homage to the infamous Stanford squirrels that were darting around during my visit.

Acorn is as much a context layer as it is a website. When we build with agents, we use markdown files in the repo so the model can carry goals, constraints, and decisions from one session to the next. Acorn acts as that durable context window, something the average person could meaningfully interact with. You can talk to your agent and have it write things down, or scratch a half-formed idea into the pad yourself and have your agent make sense of it later. Either way you are both working on the same workspace: your goals, your notes on frosh-friendly classes, and the professors who you think might give you a research opportunity -- all forming a plan built collaboratively.

The page registers 22 tools with the browser using WebMCP, giving an agent human-level control over the workspace. It searches the catalog I imported from Stanford's public course feed, 15,618 courses across 256 departments, with 2,220 real sections where each entry is an actual enrollable week, a lecture paired with its discussion. Ask for a schedule and a solver walks the real combinations, keeps the hours you blocked off, leaves you walking time between classes, plans around your club meetings, and hands the agent ranked options it can apply in one command. Your clicks and the agent's calls run through the same command layer, so everything either of you does leaves a receipt and can be undone from the interface.

The hardest question was how much to hardcode and how much to leave to the user's agent. An empty catalog is useless, but a catalog nobody can correct goes stale when anything changes. I ended up shipping verified reference data as the base and allowing edits on top of it. The student or the agent can amend (or add) any course, club, or activity. 

To test all of this, I spun up Fable agents that played the students, each of them using the agentic workflow of Codex to plan their quarters collaboratively. Those rounds of testing caught problems I would never have found clicking around myself. One agent invented an address for a page that did not exist, followed its own made-up link, and stranded itself on an empty 404 with no tools at all, so now every dead end in the app leads back to the workspace. The app told one student her AP exam was worth "1 units." Another student's research counted for real units and the degree math left them out, so her progress read lower than it actually was. Saves took 32 seconds at one point, because every save was hauling ten full copies of the workspace as undo history, so I capped the history and saves came back to about three seconds. And once the app claimed a save that never reached the server, which is the one bug you cannot ship when an agent is editing someone's degree plan, so every write now proves itself and rolls back when it cannot.

Through this, I learned that the divergence I kept running into had nothing to do with model capability. Once the browsing was replaced with typed tools and the context was durable, the same model that built my backend planned my quarter just as well.

Next, I want to ship the workspace barebones and give the agent more autonomy over it. The reference tools already let an agent research a university and build its catalog, programs, and clubs from official sources under the same provenance rules, so Acorn can extend beyond Stanford to any college, and past college to any long-running task where a person and their agent need one shared memory. The squirrels I named this after bury acorns all over campus and find them again months later, and that is what I want from planning with an agent: whatever the two of you put down, you come back and it is still there.
