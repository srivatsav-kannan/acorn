# About the project

Devpost story field. Markdown, no headings, freeflowing by design.

---

This summer I kept flipping between two kinds of work: building the backend for my startup, and figuring out what my first year at Stanford should actually look like, courses, research, clubs, all of it. I used the same frontier models for both, and the divergence hit me in the face. The model that stood up a fully authenticated backend meant to hold real user data for ten thousand people, then wired it to a frontend without blinking (CORS errors alone used to eat my evenings not so long ago), was reduced to a lost tourist the moment I pointed it at my own planning. It misclicked around Axess. It quoted stale Reddit threads and walked into broken links. It once closed out an hour of browsing by accident, and it forgot everything about me in a matter of seconds. Frontier intelligence clearly exists. Consumer AI just cannot reach it, and the two things standing in the way are context and browsing.

So when I heard WebMCP was moving forward, a concept I had heard about but never seen shipped, this hackathon landed at exactly the right moment. If the page hands the agent real, typed tools, the browsing problem dissolves. If the workspace itself holds the student's context, the amnesia goes with it. I finally got to point frontier intelligence at possibly the most important planning task of my adult life, and I named the thing Acorn, an homage to the infamous black squirrels I watched darting around campus on my visit.

Acorn is a context layer that happens to look like a degree planner. Anyone who builds with agents knows the ritual: every serious project grows a pile of markdown files carrying goals, constraints, and decisions between sessions, because the model remembers nothing on its own. Acorn is that pile rebuilt as a place a person actually likes spending time. You and your agent work the same surface. Talk through your quarter and let the agent write it down, or scratch a half-formed idea into the pad and let the agent make sense of it later. Your goals, your protected hours, your reasoning about why CS 106B goes in autumn: all of it stays put between chats, and the calendar draws the week you would actually be signing up for.

The page registers 22 tools with the browser through `document.modelContext`, and the honest engineering is in making them safe to hand to something with no intent of its own. Every mutation, a human click or an agent call, runs through the same deterministic command layer with an expected version and an idempotency key, writes a receipt, and can be undone from the interface. The data is real: a full import of Stanford's public ExploreCourses feed, 15,618 courses across 256 departments, with 2,220 sections where each entry is a genuine enrollable week, lecture paired with its discussion. Ask for a schedule and a deterministic solver enumerates real section combinations, clears your protected windows, transition buffers, and club meetings, then hands back ranked options the agent applies with one undoable command. First-call tool responses stay under 1,500 characters, because an agent's context window is a budget and a workspace that wastes it is a bad tool.

The hardest problem was drawing the line between persistence and agency: how much should I hardcode, and how much should the user's agent decide? A catalog you cannot trust is useless, and a catalog you cannot correct goes stale the day you scrape it. I landed on shipping verified reference data as the floor and letting the student or the agent amend any listing on top of it, with a source attached, the change shown against the original, and an unverified banner until someone re-checks it. Autonomy stays safe as long as nothing is silent.

Testing found the rest. I made a coding agent play the student: a Playwright bridge exposes the page's tools over HTTP, and Codex ran seventeen rounds of a synthetic freshman living in the workspace and filing complaints. Early rounds were humbling. Saves took 32 seconds because undo snapshots had swollen the payload to 1.7 MB (a diet got it to 520 KB). A lost acknowledgment made the app claim a save that never landed, which is the kind of lie that ends trust in an agent product, so writes now settle against a version probe and either adopt the server's truth or roll back loudly. An identical replayed command returned a conflict instead of its stored receipt. None of this shows up when a polite human clicks buttons, and all of it shows up in the first hour of letting a model drive, which is exactly why I let one drive. The suite ended at 363 unit tests and 38 end-to-end browser tests, all green.

What I learned is that agent ergonomics is product design. An agent is a user with infinite patience for JSON and zero patience for ambiguity, and designing for it forces the honesty a human interface lets you postpone: one command path, explicit versions, receipts for everything.

Next, the workspace ships barebones and the agent earns more autonomy over it. The reference tools already let an agent research a school and build its catalog, programs, and clubs from official sources, so the same context layer extends past Stanford to any college, and past college to any long-running task where a person and their agent need one shared memory. Stanford's black squirrels bury acorns all over campus and somehow find every one of them months later. That is the product I want: plant context anywhere, lose nothing, come back and find it exactly where you left it.
